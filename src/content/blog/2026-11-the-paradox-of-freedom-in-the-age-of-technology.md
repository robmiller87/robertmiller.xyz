Humanity has spent much of its history trying to become freer.

We have developed political institutions to protect our rights, economic systems to facilitate cooperation, medicines to liberate us from disease, and technologies that have progressively expanded the boundaries of what an individual can achieve.

The internet may be one of the most extraordinary developments in this long pursuit of freedom. Never before have so many people had access to so much information, so many opportunities, or so many ways to participate in the world beyond their immediate surroundings.

I am an enormous beneficiary of this progress.

Technology has allowed me to build a career across countries and industries, access knowledge that would once have been reserved for a privileged few, and develop a degree of financial and geographical independence that I could scarcely have imagined growing up.

I love technology. I wouldn't be living the life I have today without it.

And yet, increasingly, I find myself confronting a peculiar contradiction.

**The same technologies that have given us unprecedented freedom have also become extraordinarily effective at persuading us to surrender it.**

Not through coercion. Not through the threat of punishment or the removal of our rights.

But through pleasure.

## The freedom to choose

When I was around fourteen, I developed a habit of retreating to my bedroom, lying flat on my bed, and thinking.

There was plenty going on around me. School, friends, girlfriends, and the usual uncertainties of adolescence. Home could be hectic, too.

I grew up around alcohol and drugs. I saw how easily certain habits could come to dominate people's lives, and how difficult it could be to escape them once they had taken hold.

I wasn't immune to the temptations myself. But I was determined not to drift into a life I hadn't consciously chosen.

Those moments alone were an opportunity to examine the direction I was heading in. To think about the man I wanted to become, or perhaps more accurately, the man I was determined not to become.

I was giving myself what I might now describe as a healthy dose of paranoia.

Was I making the right decisions? Were the people and habits around me influencing me in ways I didn't want? Was I still heading in the right direction?

Looking back, I think I was developing an early appreciation for something that has become increasingly important to me: personal sovereignty.

Not sovereignty in the conventional political sense, but the ability to remain the principal author of one's own life.

Around the same time, I developed an aversion to mobile phones and, later, Facebook.

It wasn't because I disliked people. I was sociable, enjoyed conversation, and felt comfortable meeting people in the real world.

Something simply felt wrong about the prospect of creating a digital representation of myself, curating photographs, maintaining a profile, and allowing that representation to influence how others perceived me.

I didn't want to spend my time managing a version of myself for public consumption.

I wanted to get on with living.

I certainly couldn't have articulated the implications of the attention economy at fourteen. But I recognize something of the same instinct today.

An unease about allowing external systems to determine how I spend my time, what I pay attention to, and ultimately who I become.

## The technological bargain

Of course, the modern digital economy offers an extraordinary bargain.

In exchange for our attention, we receive entertainment, information, social connection, convenience, and access to opportunities that previous generations could scarcely imagine.

For the most part, we enter into this bargain willingly.

Nobody needs to force us to open Instagram, watch another YouTube video, or check the financial markets for the twentieth time in a day.

We do it ourselves.

We might complain about how much time we spend on our phones. We might feel slightly dissatisfied after an evening of scrolling. We might wish we had read a book, exercised, worked on something meaningful, or spent more time with the people we love.

But for many of us, the pleasure of the next distraction remains more compelling than the discomfort of the time we've already lost.

And so we continue.

This is what makes the relationship between individuals and the technological industrial complex so interesting.

The companies that dominate our digital lives have powerful economic incentives to keep us engaged.

Attention can be converted into advertising revenue, transactions, subscriptions, data, and commercial influence. The more frequently we return, the more opportunities there are to monetize our participation.

Not every technology company operates according to this model, and not every minute of engagement is harmful. But for many digital platforms, the incentive to capture and retain attention is deeply embedded in the business.

The result is a system in which enormous quantities of capital, engineering talent, and computational power are devoted to making digital experiences increasingly difficult to disengage from.

Yet it would be too convenient to place all the blame on the companies.

They are responding, in part, to something deeply human.

Our appetite for novelty, pleasure, social validation, stimulation, and the possibility of reward.

The technological industrial complex has become exceptionally good at satisfying those appetites.

And most of us are willing participants.

## Pleasure, purpose, and the architecture of dependence

Many of the things that make life extraordinary also have the potential to consume us.

Food nourishes us and brings people together. Sexuality creates intimacy and pleasure. Money allows us to exchange value, cooperate, and pursue opportunities. Medicines relieve suffering. Entertainment offers enjoyment, relaxation, and escape.

These are gifts of human existence.

But they can also become objects of excessive consumption, compulsive behaviour, or dependence.

The problem isn't pleasure itself.

It's what happens when our pursuit of immediate gratification begins to undermine the things we value over the longer term.

I've experienced versions of this tension throughout my life.

There were periods when alcohol became a way of escaping the difficulties of work and everyday life. Other periods when digital stimulation has occupied far more of my attention than I wanted them to.

Over time, I made deliberate changes to those relationships, including giving up alcohol altogether.

But perhaps the most interesting example is trading.

I discovered financial markets roughly a decade ago, and over the years trading has become an extraordinary source of opportunity and independence.

It has helped me create a life with far more control over my time than I would have enjoyed in a conventional career.

I remain deeply grateful for that.

But financial markets are also an almost perfect environment for capturing human attention.

They are available around the clock, filled with uncertainty, and capable of delivering immediate financial and emotional rewards.

There is always another price movement to examine, another opportunity to investigate, another piece of information that might change everything.

And when your livelihood depends on those markets, it becomes remarkably easy to justify almost any amount of attention devoted to them.

Research becomes entertainment. Monitoring becomes compulsion. The distinction between productive activity and distraction becomes increasingly difficult to maintain.

The irony isn't lost on me.

**I pursued financial independence partly to gain control over my time, only to discover that the very activity providing that independence could become one of the greatest consumers of it.**

Which raises a question that extends far beyond financial markets.

At what point does a technology that enables our freedom become something we need freedom from?

## The illusion of sovereignty

For years, I've been interested in the idea that technological progress will gradually redistribute power away from traditional institutions, including nation-states, and toward individuals and networks.

Digital finance, remote work, global communication, and increasingly accessible computational capabilities all offer new ways for people to operate beyond the constraints of geography and conventional institutions.

I remain sympathetic to that vision.

But I've become increasingly conscious that sovereignty has at least two dimensions.

There is external sovereignty: the freedom to choose where we live, how we earn money, what we create, and which institutions we depend upon.

And there is internal sovereignty: the ability to direct our own attention, regulate our impulses, and act consistently with our considered intentions.

The two are related, but they are not the same.

Someone can possess considerable financial independence while remaining psychologically captive to the markets.

Someone can have access to more knowledge than any previous generation while struggling to concentrate long enough to read a book.

Someone can have complete freedom over their calendar while allowing digital platforms to determine how much of that time is actually spent.

**What good is having sovereignty over the circumstances of our lives if we struggle to exercise sovereignty over our own behaviour?**

There is another complication.

When power moves away from traditional institutions, it doesn't necessarily move toward individuals.

It can move toward corporations, platforms, networks, and the owners of technological infrastructure.

We might become less dependent on one set of institutions while becoming profoundly dependent on another.

And unlike traditional political authority, these newer forms of influence often operate through convenience and desire rather than explicit rules or coercion.

We don't necessarily experience them as restrictions on our freedom.

We experience them as products we enjoy using.

## A minority pursuit

I don't expect this tension to disappear.

Nor do I believe that the majority of people will suddenly reject the digital environments they have become accustomed to.

Most people are probably somewhat dissatisfied with the amount of time they spend distracted.

But dissatisfaction alone rarely produces change.

The immediate pleasures of entertainment, stimulation, and convenience are often more powerful than the distant, abstract costs of lost attention.

And perhaps, for many people, the exchange is perfectly acceptable.

Not everybody shares my conception of a purposeful life. Nor should they.

But I suspect that for a minority, the desire to exercise greater control over their attention will become increasingly important.

These people won't necessarily reject technology. In fact, they may be among its greatest enthusiasts.

They'll simply become more selective about the role they allow it to play in their lives.

We can already see expressions of this instinct in the growing interest in digital minimalism, distraction-free writing tools, dedicated reading devices, dumb phones, and alternative approaches to personal computing.

Products like the Kindle, reMarkable, and emerging e-paper displays suggest that there is an appetite for devices that perform particular functions without attempting to become the center of our digital existence.

E-paper is especially interesting to me.

It offers the possibility of presenting useful information in a quiet, persistent, low-power format, without many of the characteristics we've come to associate with conventional screens.

But the technology itself isn't the answer.

An e-paper device can be just as distracting as a smartphone if it's designed around the same incentives.

What interests me is the philosophy behind the design.

The possibility of creating tools that deliver information without demanding engagement.

Technology that allows us to remain connected to the things we care about without continually pulling us away from the lives we're trying to live.

## Choosing our relationship with technology

I have no desire to return to a world without smartphones, the internet, or digital financial markets.

Technology has given me an extraordinary amount of freedom. I love what it makes possible, and I remain optimistic about its capacity to improve human life.

But I am far less optimistic about the likelihood that the dominant digital business models will voluntarily begin prioritizing human fulfillment over commercial engagement.

The incentives are too powerful, and our appetite for distraction too deeply human.

I suspect the pursuit of a more deliberate relationship with technology will remain relatively esoteric.

Perhaps it will always be the concern of a minority.

But that's not necessarily a reason to dismiss it.

Lately, I've found myself returning to ideas I've been thinking about for years: dedicated information devices, calmer interfaces, and technologies that allow us to access the benefits of connectivity without constantly inviting us back into the digital world.

I'm not trying to persuade the world to abandon technology, nor do I believe we can engineer away the human appetite for distraction.

I'm interested in building for people who have already begun questioning the bargain.

People who love technology, recognize its extraordinary power, and nevertheless want to retain greater control over the direction of their lives.

When I think back to that fourteen-year-old lying on his bed, examining his choices and worrying about the man he might become, I recognize the same instinct.

The world has changed enormously since then. The tools are more powerful, the opportunities greater, and the demands on our attention almost unimaginable by the standards of my childhood.

But the underlying question hasn't changed very much.

**Am I directing my life, or am I allowing the world around me to direct it for me?**

I don't expect technology to answer that question on our behalf.

But I believe we can build tools that make it a little easier to answer for ourselves.
